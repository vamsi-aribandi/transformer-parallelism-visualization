A Visual Guide to Parallel Transformers

How a transformer's forward and backward passes are sharded across devices — data, tensor, context, pipeline, and expert parallelism, step by step, in the notation of the JAX scaling book.

How to read the notation

Everything here is written in the scaling book's sharding notation, and it takes one sentence to learn: a subscript on a dimension names the mesh axis it is split over. The devices form a mesh — Mesh({'X': 4}) is four devices along one axis named X — and In[BX,  T,  D] means the batch dimension B is sharded four ways over X, so each device holds a [B/4,  T,  D] slice. A dimension with no subscript is not split: every device sees all of it. A tensor with no subscripts anywhere is fully replicated.

One more piece of notation: a trailing {UX} marks an unreduced partial sum — each device holds a full-shaped tensor that is only its contribution to the true result, and the shards must still be summed over X (that sum is exactly what AllReduce and ReduceScatter do). The dimension names used throughout, with the concrete sizes the tooltips assume: B=8 sequences, T=128 tokens each, D=1024 the model (residual) width, F=4096 the MLP hidden width, H=1024 the fused attention head dimension (all heads concatenated — not the head count), and for the MoE section E=4 experts with S=256 tokens routed to each. Everything is bf16.

How to read the figures

Each figure is a small machine you can drive. Model depth runs left to right: the input enters at In, passes through each layer's attention and MLP stations, and leaves at Out. Devices are horizontal lanes, stacked vertically — when devices communicate, tensors fly vertically between lanes. Weights are blue, activations amber, keys and values teal, and gradients rose. A dashed outline is the ghost of a tensor's full logical extent: the solid patch inside it is the slice this device holds, drawn at that device's actual offset. Hover any tensor for its shape, sharding, and memory at our nominal sizes (B=8, T=128, D=1024, F=4096, bf16).

The step bar under each figure is discrete — every segment is one semantic operation (a matmul, a collective, a save). Click a segment to jump to the state after that operation; press play to watch the operations animate in order. Whenever the current operation is a collective, a panel beneath the canvas shows how it actually runs on the wire — the bidirectional ring pattern from the scaling book, hop by hop. The program beside the canvas is the whole pass as a list of operations in scaling-book notation — it follows along as you step, and clicking any line jumps straight there. Each figure opens in forward-only mode; switch to + Backward to watch the full training step, where activations get saved beneath the weights that will need them, and every backward operation cites the forward operation it differentiates.

The axes of transformer sharding

Every way of splitting a transformer across devices is a choice of which dimension to shard: the batch, the sequence, the weights' features, the experts, or the layers. This section takes them one at a time, each on its own four-device mesh, so the communication each one costs is visible in isolation. The next section combines them the way real training runs do.

Data parallelism

The simplest strategy: replicate the weights everywhere and shard the batch — each device gets In[BX,  T,  D], a quarter of the sequences, and runs the whole model on them. The forward pass needs zero communication; step through it and watch nothing ever cross between lanes. The price appears in the backward pass: every device computes a weight gradient from only its shard of the batch, so the gradients are unreduced partial sums — dW[D,  F]{UX} — and each one must be AllReduced across the data axis before the optimizer can step. That per-weight AllReduce is the classic data-parallel gradient sync.

ZeRO-1: shard the optimizer, keep the weights

Plain data parallelism replicates more than the weights: every device also keeps a full copy of the optimizer state, which for Adam is two more tensors the size of the model. ZeRO-1 keeps the weights replicated but shards the optimizer state over the data axis, and that changes only the gradient step. Instead of AllReducing each dW[D,  F]{UX}, it ReduceScatters it — ReduceScatterX,D — so each device holds one slice, dW[DX,  F]. Each device then updates just its slice of the weight, W[DX,  F], and an AllGather of the updated slices makes the weights whole again, W[D,  F]. Watch the wire panel: an AllReduce is a ReduceScatter followed by an AllGather, so ZeRO-1 moves the same bytes as data parallelism — it has only pulled the optimizer step in between the two halves. The forward pass is untouched. This is the data axis DeepSeek-V3 and Kimi K2 train with.

Fully-sharded data parallelism (ZeRO-3)

ZeRO-1 sharded the optimizer state but still replicated every weight four times — FSDP (ZeRO-3) refuses to pay that memory either. Weights are sharded along the same data axis (Win[DX,  F], Wout[F,  DX]), and each one is AllGathered just in time, used for its matmul, and immediately discarded — watch the station header switch to the gathered shape and back. The backward pass pays the gather again (the weight is long gone), and the gradient flows the other way: each dW is a partial sum that ReduceScatters back to exactly the shard layout the weights live in — memory of a shard, communication of a gather, in both passes.

Tensor parallelism

Tensor parallelism (the Megatron scheme) shards the feature dimensions: attention heads across devices (Wqkv[D,  HY], Wo[HY,  D]) and the MLP's hidden width (Win[D,  FY], Wout[FY,  D]). Activations travel sharded on D. Every block then follows one rhythm: AllGather on the way in, ReduceScatter on the way out. The first matmul needs the full D, so the sharded activations are gathered; the second matmul contracts a sharded dimension, so each device is left holding an unreduced partial sum — the dashed {UY} tensor — which the ReduceScatter resolves while re-sharding for the next block.

Watch the backward pass mirror the forward exactly: the gradient arrives where the output left, the forward's ReduceScatter becomes an AllGather of dOut, the forward's AllGather becomes a ReduceScatter of dX, and each weight's gradient (dW = X⊤ · dY) pulses the activation that was saved beneath it. Weight gradients stay local — in pure TP nothing sums across the batch, because every device saw the whole batch.

Context parallelism

Long sequences don't fit on one device, so shard the sequence: In[B,  TX,  D], each device holding a quarter of the tokens. The MLP never notices — tokens are independent there, so it runs without a word between devices. Attention is the whole story: every query must see every key and value, so K and V are AllGathered across the sequence shards while Q stays local. The gathered K/V are used and dropped, not kept: saving full-sequence tensors on every device would be the exact memory blowup this strategy exists to avoid, so the forward saves only each device's own K/V shard and the backward pass re-gathers them — the same trade FSDP makes with weights. Then the gathers come back to haunt us a second way: every device produces gradient contributions for all tokens' keys and values, so dK and dV are partial sums that ReduceScatter back over the sequence — and the weight gradients AllReduce over the context axis, exactly like data parallelism sums over the batch.

Expert parallelism

In a mixture-of-experts layer the MLP becomes four experts, one per device (Win[EZ,  D,  F]), and each token is routed to one of them. The figure's tokens are small squares colored by their assigned expert: the AllToAll dispatch is the moment every device sends every other device the tokens that belong to it — watch the colors sort themselves into lanes. The experts run an ordinary MLP on their guests, and a second AllToAll sends every token home. The backward pass mirrors it: token gradients AllToAll out to the experts, expert weight gradients stay local (each expert owns its weights outright), and the attention weight gradients AllReduce over the batch axis as in data parallelism.

Pipeline parallelism

Pipeline parallelism shards the layers: stage 0 owns layer 1, stage 1 owns layer 2, and activations hop across the boundary with a single point-to-point send — the cheapest communication in this whole article. The catch is idleness: with one batch, each stage would wait on the other, so the batch splits into four microbatches that chase each other through the pipe. The Gantt chart under the lanes fills as you step: the staircase is the pipeline filling, the empty corners are the bubble. Switch to + Backward and the gradients flow right-to-left through the same pipe — each backward cell is drawn twice as wide because backward costs roughly twice the compute, which is why the bubble grows during training.

Examples from frontier models

Training reports rarely use one of these alone. Reading the recent ones — DeepSeek-V3 and V4, Kimi K2, GLM-4.5/5, Nemotron-4, -H and 3, Qwen3-VL, and Llama 3 — the recipes fall into three families. The figures below show each one with two devices per axis so the grids stay readable; the real degrees are in the text. The canvas changes shape here: each pipeline stage is a grid of devices, arranged so that every mesh axis is a fixed direction — an FSDP partner is always two rows away, a tensor-parallel partner the next column, and so on — and every collective flies along exactly one of them, wrapped in that axis's color, with a badge and a program tag naming the parallelism responsible. The tally under the step bar counts collectives per axis.

Dense models: Llama 3

Dense models use 4D: FSDP × TP × CP × PP. Llama 3 405B trained with TP 8, PP 16 and FSDP 64 at 8K context, then TP 8, CP 16, PP 16 and FSDP 8 for the 128K stage — context parallelism is switched on only when the sequences get long. The dimensions are ordered by bandwidth: TP stays inside a server, DP spans the cluster. Nemotron-4 340B (TP 8 × PP 12 × DP) and Nemotron-H (TP 8 × DP 768, no PP) are the same family without the context axis. Here: Mesh({'X': 2, 'Y': 2, 'C': 2, 'stage': 2}), activations In[BX,  TC,  DY]. Every matmul opens with FSDP's weight gather and TP's activation gather; CP only speaks in attention; the pipeline is one send per direction.

Open MoE models: DeepSeek-V3 and Kimi K2

Open MoE models use 3D: EP × PP × ZeRO-1, and skip TP. DeepSeek-V3 trained with PP 16 (DualPipe), EP 64 across 8 nodes and ZeRO-1 data parallelism, explicitly "without costly tensor parallelism"; Kimi K2 with PP 16, EP 16 and ZeRO-1; GLM-4.5V with EP 8 × PP 4, adding CP 4 only for its long-context stage. Here: Mesh({'X': 2, 'Z': 2, 'stage': 2}), activations In[BXZ,  T,  D] — the batch is split over both the data axis and the expert axis, because outside the MoE block the expert axis is just more data parallelism. The forward pass has no gathers at all: only the AllToAllZ pair per MoE layer and the stage hop. The backward is where the two data-like axes show their difference: an attention weight gradient scatters over X (ZeRO-1) and sums over Z (the expert axis replicates attention), while an expert weight gradient only scatters over X — each expert owns its weights. The optimizer then steps on each shard and the updated weights gather back.

Everything at once: Nemotron 3 and Qwen3-VL

Megatron-based labs use everything at once. Nemotron 3 Nano's long-context phase ran CP 8 × TP 8 × EP 8 × PP 4 (Nemotron 3 Ultra pushes EP to 128), and Qwen3-VL lists TP, PP, CP, EP and ZeRO-1 DP on up to 10,000 GPUs. Here it is with two of everything: 32 devices, Mesh({'X': 2, 'Y': 2, 'C': 2, 'Z': 2, 'stage': 2}), activations In[BXZ,  TC,  DY] — sharded four ways at once. Each stage is a 4×4 grid; rows are X then C, columns Z then Y. The tally row is the takeaway: FSDP and TP do most of the talking, CP speaks only in attention, EP is two AllToAlls per MoE layer, and PP is one send per direction. In the backward, a weight gradient has to be summed over every axis that split the tokens — X, Z and C — which is why one gradient takes three collectives to settle.

Notation and shardings follow the JAX scaling book. Figures are recorded from the same source of truth as the companion videos; collective sequences are pinned by tests against the book's four sharded-matmul cases.
